# Monroe Stephenson

Monroe Stephenson is a backend engineer and mathematician living in Berlin. He works on Superchat's AI team in Kotlin and Quarkus, building voice agents, chatbots, and automation over a unified business inbox. His broader engineering interests include distributed systems, database internals, network telemetry, streaming algorithms, and useful AI systems that operate under real memory, privacy, and infrastructure constraints.

## Background

Monroe read mathematics at Reed College and worked in particle physics, network topology, commutative algebra, and combinatorics at research groups in the United States, Germany, and Israel. A Fulbright research grant brought him to the Max Planck Institute for Mathematics in the Sciences, where he worked with Bernd Sturmfels on algebraic statistics and partitioned independent component analysis. He is completing an M.S. in Computer Science at Georgia Tech with an emphasis on security, networks, operating systems, and database systems.

## Research

His publications and preprints include work on cumulant tensors in partitioned independent component analysis, total p-anisotropy for simplicial cycles, and differential powers of ideals. Earlier research covers DDoS mitigation through the Abelian sandpile model and detector work for the LDMX light-dark-matter experiment. Current experimental work appears under [Cranked Mathematics](https://gramscian.com/cranked), an effort to use AI agents for search, computation, attempted proofs, and formalization without treating model output as verification. The project studies both the mathematics itself and the boundary between plausible generation and dependable proof.

## Software

Public projects include [opn](https://github.com/monroestephenson/opn), a structured-output network-telemetry command-line tool; [FlowSketch](https://github.com/monroestephenson/flowsketch), which explores bounded-memory sketches over packet streams; and [The Long Inheritance](https://gramscian.com/literature/), an interactive map of literary influence across four thousand years.
